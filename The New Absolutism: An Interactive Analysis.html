The New Absolutism

An interactive analysis of how modern identity ideologies, from Communism to Wokeness, endanger open societies.

Our political landscape is fractured. Debates that once revolved around policy and economics have been replaced by intense, often vicious, culture wars. An analysis of the dominant ideologies of our time—tracing a line from 20th-century Communism through modern Feminism, LGBTQIA+ activism, and the contemporary phenomenon of "Wokeness"—reveals that this is no accident.

These movements, despite their different goals, share a deep structural DNA that is increasingly at odds with the foundational principles of a tolerant, open society. While the horrors of state Communism seem a world away from a corporate DEI seminar, a close examination reveals a shared architecture. This architecture functions like an ideological mind virusA self-replicating system of thought that prioritizes its own propagation over truth and fosters division as a means of control., a system that is essential to understand if we are to diagnose the modern problems of social polarization, the decay of rational discourse, and the rise of a new, decentralized form of absolutism.

The Shared Logic: From Class War to Culture War

The 20th century's most destructive ideology, Communism, was built on a simple, powerful premise: all of history is a struggle between an oppressor class and an oppressed class. This binary framework, dividing the world into a righteous in-group and a demonized out-group, proved remarkably effective for mobilizing revolutionary fervor. This same core logic has been adapted and redeployed by subsequent movements, shifting the battleground from economic class to cultural identity.

Communism

Core Conflict: Bourgeoisie (oppressor) vs. Proletariat (oppressed).

Framework: Views history as a material struggle over the means of production. The utopian goal of a classless society justified the violent overthrow of the state and the negation of individual rights.

Radical Feminism

Core Conflict: Patriarchy/Men (oppressor) vs. Women (oppressed).

Framework: Adapts the Marxist dialectic, substituting economic class with gender. History is framed as a relentless conflict over social and reproductive power.

Queer Theory

Core Conflict: Normativity (oppressor) vs. The "Queer" (oppressed).

Framework: Further abstracts the conflict to a struggle against social norms (heteronormativity, cisnormativity). The goal is to deconstruct the very categories of identity.

Wokeness / Critical Social Justice

Core Conflict: Privileged Groups (oppressor) vs. Marginalized Groups (oppressed).

Framework: Creates a complex matrix of interlocking oppressions based on identity. "Privilege" is the new capital in a perpetual, zero-sum conflict over cultural and social power.

The Intellectual DNA

The parallels between these ideologies are not coincidental; they reveal a direct intellectual lineage. The core logic of Marxism—a binary dialectic of an oppressor class exploiting an oppressed class over resources—has been systematically adapted.

  • Second-wave feminism explicitly adopted this structure, substituting economic class with gender and casting patriarchy as the oppressor.
  • Queer Theory further abstracted the terms to a conflict between the "normative" and the "deviant," with the struggle being over the power to define social reality itself.
  • Wokeness and Critical Race Theory retain this fundamental dialectic but fractalize it into a matrix of interlocking conflicts based on identity, where "privilege" becomes the new form of capital.

This shared DNA explains the frequent political alignment of these ideologies and their similar rhetorical strategies, which are grounded in group-based grievance and aimed at revolutionary or deconstructive goals.

The "Inward Turn": From Material Facts to Subjective Truth

A crucial evolution has occurred in how these ideologies define the problem. Communism located its struggle in the material world: the ownership of factories. Modern identity movements have taken a profound "inward turn," shifting the battleground to the intangible and psychological.

This shift has monumental consequences. When the problem is an invisible system like "patriarchy," and the primary evidence is an individual's "lived experience" of oppression, the entire basis of rational debate changes.

This leads to two powerful ideological tools:

  • Standpoint TheoryThe claim that individuals from marginalized groups have a more authentic understanding of reality due to their experiences of oppression.: This creates a "hierarchy of victimhood" where moral authority is determined by identity, not the validity of an argument.
  • The Weaponization of "Harm": Dissenting opinions are no longer merely incorrect; they are framed as acts of "violence" or "erasure," making good-faith debate impossible and justifying censorship.

The New Propaganda: From State Control to Social Contagion

The methods of ideological propagation have also transformed. While Communism relied on a top-down, state-controlled machine, modern movements have perfected a decentralized, networked model of social contagionA phenomenon where ideas, emotions, and behaviors spread rapidly and often unconsciously through social networks, much like a virus..

Top-Down Monolith (Communism)

  • State-controlled media (Pravda)
  • Systematic indoctrination in schools
  • Pervasive censorship and terror
  • Goal: Total information monopoly

Bottom-Up Network (Modern Ideologies)

  • Hashtag activism (#MeToo)
  • Institutional capture (DEI)
  • Cancel CulturePublic shaming campaigns to de-platform or fire individuals who violate ideological norms. & social enforcement
  • Goal: Cultural saturation via peer pressure

Today, social media platforms, with algorithms optimized for engagement, create the perfect environment for this new propaganda. Outrage spreads virally, driven by emotion rather than reason, creating perpetual conflict cycles and extreme social polarization.

The Framework Test: A Reflection

Many people are drawn to these frameworks through a genuine desire for justice. But do the core principles, when followed to their logical conclusions, lead to a more open and tolerant society? Take this short test to reflect on your own framework.

Answer a few simple questions to begin.

A Framework for Justice

Your answers suggest you have a strong framework for understanding the world. You likely believe in identifying power imbalances, listening to the experiences of the vulnerable, striving for a more equitable future, and understanding that words have power. These are the foundations of many movements for social justice.

The Logical Conclusions

Here is how those same, well-intentioned principles can become the basis for absolutist ideologies.

Because you believe history is a struggle between groups, you might see the logic in the Marxist view that all private property is a tool of that struggle and must be abolished for the collective good, even by force.

Your agreement that personal experience is key to understanding injustice aligns with tactics that can dismiss rational debate, framing any disagreement with a "lived experience" as an act of personal harm or invalidation.

Your desire for a truly equitable society connects to the idea of "equity" over "equality," which has been used to justify policies that treat individuals differently based on their group identity to correct for historical imbalances, a practice at odds with principles of equal opportunity.

Your belief that language can be a tool of oppression is the foundation for modern "cancel culture," where individuals can be de-platformed or fired for using words deemed harmful, regardless of their intent, chilling free speech.

A Final Question for Reflection:

At what point does a framework for justice become a rigid ideology that creates new injustices? Does the path to a promised utopia justify the erosion of individual rights, free speech, and open debate along the way?

The Paradox of Liberation

The greatest paradox is that the tangible successes of movements like Feminism and LGBTQIA+ activism have not led to greater social harmony. As their concrete legal goals—the right to vote, to work, to marry—were achieved, the movements' focus shifted to more abstract and totalizing goals: dismantling "patriarchy," deconstructing the "gender binary," or eradicating "systemic whiteness."

A demand for voting rights is a finite political objective. A demand to dismantle "patriarchy" is an infinite cultural and psychological project that implicates every person's thoughts, language, and behavior. The battleground moves from the statehouse to the soul.

This creates a world of perpetual conflict with no possibility of forgiveness. If the problem is an immutable characteristic like being male or white, and the sin is "privilege," there is no path to redemption, only endless confession and struggle. This framing erodes social cohesion and undermines the principles of universalism and shared humanity that are the bedrock of any functioning, diverse society.

Conclusion: The Dangers of the New Absolutism

The ultimate danger of these ideologies lies in their absolutism. By claiming to possess a total truth that explains all of society's problems and by dividing the world into a morally pure in-group and a demonized out-group, they shut down the possibility of reason, debate, and progress. Communism showed the world the catastrophic consequences of such an ideology when fused with the absolute power of the state.

The critical challenge of the 21st century is to recognize and resist the same ideological architecture as it operates through the decentralized, contagious, and psychologically potent channels of our modern digital world, before it inflicts irreparable damage on the culture of liberty itself.
